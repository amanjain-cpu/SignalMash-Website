Toll-Free Verification: How It Works, How Long It Takes, and Why It Gets Rejected

Quick answer: how toll-free verification works in 2026

Before a US toll-free number (800, 888, 877, 866, 855, 844 or 833) can send business text messages, it has to be verified. You submit a verified-sender form through your messaging provider covering your legal business details, your use case, your expected volume, sample messages and — the part that decides most outcomes — your opt-in workflow and the URL where it can be seen. Approval usually takes one to two weeks, though a rejection and resubmission can push that to three or four. Unverified toll-free traffic is filtered or blocked outright, not merely surcharged. There is no trust score and no tiered throughput as there is with 10DLC: a verified toll-free number is either approved or it is not, and typically runs around three to ten messages per second.

Toll-free verification looks like a formality until it is not. Most teams submit the form, assume it is a rubber stamp, and then discover a fortnight later that their launch date has moved because a reviewer could not find the opt-in checkbox on the page they linked to. The process itself is not difficult. It is just unforgiving about details that nobody tells you matter.

This guide covers what you actually submit, how long each stage takes, the reasons submissions come back rejected, and what to do when yours is stuck.

Why toll-free numbers need verification at all

Toll-free numbers were a messaging loophole for years. They carried business text traffic without the registration that local numbers required, which made them the obvious home for senders who wanted to avoid scrutiny. Carriers closed that gap: toll-free messaging now runs through a verification process, and unverified traffic is treated as untrusted.

The practical consequence is that verification is not optional and not retroactive. Sending first and verifying later does not work, because the filtering starts immediately and a history of blocked traffic does not help your submission.

What you get in return is genuinely valuable. A verified toll-free number carries strong deliverability, no trust-score tiers to climb, and nationwide presence without a geographic area code. For customer service, notifications and account alerts aimed at a national audience, it is often a better fit than a local number.

What you actually submit

The verified-sender form varies slightly between providers, but the substance is standard across the industry:

  • Legal business details. Company name exactly as registered, address, website and a contact email on your business domain. A personal email address is a common and avoidable cause of delay.
  • Use case. What the messages are for — customer care, notifications, two-factor authentication, marketing, or a mix. This has to match what you actually send.
  • Expected volume. Monthly message volume and peak sending rate.
  • Sample messages. Real examples, including your business name and opt-out language.
  • Opt-in workflow and its URL. How subscribers consent, described in words, plus a link where a reviewer can see it for themselves.
  • Privacy policy and terms. Public URLs, reachable without a login.

The opt-in evidence is the part that decides most submissions. Everything else is administrative; the opt-in is the thing a reviewer actively goes looking for and frequently cannot find.

How long does toll-free verification take?

For a complete, clean submission, approval typically lands within one to two weeks. That is faster than 10DLC brand-plus-campaign registration in most cases, and dramatically faster than a short code, which runs eight to twelve weeks.

The number that catches teams out is the resubmission timeline. A rejection does not send you to the front of the queue when you fix it — you rejoin the back of it. One rejection realistically turns a two-week timeline into three or four weeks, which is usually the difference between launching on schedule and not.

That asymmetry is the entire reason to take the first submission seriously. The cost of getting it right first time is an hour of care. The cost of getting it wrong is a fortnight.

Why toll-free verifications get rejected

Rejections cluster into a small number of recurring causes.

The opt-in cannot be verified. By far the most common. The URL submitted points at a home page rather than the actual form, the form sits behind a login, the consent checkbox is pre-ticked, or the disclosure language is missing. A reviewer who cannot see consent being collected will not approve the submission.

The disclosure language is incomplete. Consent text needs to say more than "I agree to receive updates". It should name your business, say that the messages are SMS or text messages, describe what they will be about, give a frequency expectation, state that message and data rates may apply, and explain how to opt out.

Sample messages do not match the use case. Promotional samples submitted under a customer-care use case is the classic version. Reviewers read the samples against the stated purpose.

Business details do not reconcile. A trading name that does not match the registered entity, a website that does not mention the business, or a contact address on a free email domain.

Restricted content. Cannabis, firearms, gambling, high-risk lending and similar categories face carrier restrictions regardless of how clean the rest of the submission is.

The same failure modes appear in 10DLC campaign reviews, which is why the campaign approval checklist is worth reading even if toll-free is the only number type you plan to use.

What happens if you send unverified

Unverified toll-free traffic is filtered aggressively and increasingly blocked at the carrier. The messages that do get through are unreliable enough that you cannot build a customer-facing process on them — appointment reminders that arrive sometimes are arguably worse than none at all, because your customers stop trusting the channel.

There is also a reputational cost that outlasts the block. Sustained filtered traffic from a number is a signal, and cleaning that up takes longer than doing the verification properly at the start.

Toll-free or 10DLC?

Verification speed is a real reason to pick toll-free, but it should not be the only one.

Choose toll-free when your audience is national, the traffic is customer service, notifications or alerts, you want the same number to work for both calls and texts, and you would rather clear a single verification than manage a trust score.

Choose 10DLC when a local area code matters to your customers, the messaging is conversational, or you want the lowest per-message cost and are willing to work through brand and campaign registration to get it.

If your verification is stuck

Most providers offer toll-free verification; what differs is how much of it they do for you. A self-service platform hands you the form and returns the outcome. If the submission is rejected, the reason you receive is often a category rather than an explanation, and working out which of the five causes above applies is left to you. That is where two-week timelines quietly become six-week ones.

People also ask

How long does toll-free verification take?

A complete submission is usually approved within one to two weeks. A rejection means fixing the issue and rejoining the queue, which typically extends the total to three or four weeks. Toll-free verification is generally faster than 10DLC brand and campaign registration, and far faster than a short code at eight to twelve weeks.

Why was my toll-free verification rejected?

The most common reason by some margin is that the opt-in could not be verified: the URL pointed at a home page instead of the form, the form sat behind a login, or the consent disclosure was missing required elements. Other frequent causes are sample messages that do not match the stated use case, business details that do not reconcile with public records, and restricted content categories.

Can I send toll-free texts without verification?

Not reliably. Unverified toll-free traffic is filtered and increasingly blocked at the carrier, so delivery becomes inconsistent rather than merely surcharged. A record of filtered traffic also makes the eventual verification harder, so sending first and verifying later costs more than doing it in order.

Is toll-free verification the same as 10DLC registration?

No. They are separate processes for different number types. 10DLC requires registering a Brand and one or more Campaigns with The Campaign Registry, and assigns a trust score that sets your throughput. Toll-free uses a single verified-sender submission with no trust score and no tiered throughput — it is approved or it is not.

Do I need to verify a toll-free number I only use for voice?

No. Verification applies to text messaging. A toll-free number used purely for inbound calls does not need it — but if you later want customers to text the same number they call, verification has to be completed before that traffic will deliver.

What throughput does a verified toll-free number get?

Typically in the range of three to ten messages per second, which is more than sufficient for most customer-service and notification traffic. It is lower than a dedicated short code, which can push hundreds per second, and unlike 10DLC there are no trust-score tiers to climb — the rate does not improve or degrade based on a score.
