What Is RCS Business Messaging? A Plain-English Explainer for 2026

Quick answer: what RCS business messaging is

RCS business messaging is the business version of Rich Communication Services, the messaging standard that replaces SMS in the default messaging app on a phone. Instead of 160 characters from an anonymous number, a message arrives from a verified sender — your brand name, logo and a verification indicator — and can carry images, carousels, tappable reply buttons, and links, with delivery and read receipts reported back to you. It runs over data through the carrier's RCS network rather than the SMS network, needs no app download, and is supported on Android and, since Apple added RCS in iOS 18 in late 2024, on iPhone through the major US carriers as well.

Where it sits between SMS and an app

Think of three tiers. SMS is universal, plain text, and anonymous — a phone number with no identity attached. A messaging app like WhatsApp is rich and branded, but only reaches people who have installed it and agreed to be contacted there. RCS sits between the two: it has the reach of the phone's built-in messaging app and the richness of a chat app, without asking anyone to install anything.

The practical difference customers notice is identity. An SMS from a 10-digit number is indistinguishable from a scam; an RCS message shows your verified brand name and logo at the top of the thread. That single change is why the channel exists, and why the fraud-sensitive verticals — banking, delivery, healthcare, travel — adopted it first.

What you can actually send

  • Verified sender identity — brand name, logo, brand colour and a verification indicator in the thread.
  • Rich cards — an image or video with a title, description and buttons, in a single message.
  • Carousels — several swipeable cards, useful for product lists, appointment slots or delivery options.
  • Suggested replies and actions — tappable chips that reply, open a URL, dial a number, open a map or add a calendar entry, so the customer does not type.
  • Longer messages — no 160-character segmenting, so no truncation or split-message billing surprises.
  • Delivery and read receipts, and typing indicators — you can tell whether a message was actually seen, which SMS never told you.

How it differs from SMS in practice

Three things change once you move a programme to RCS. First, measurement: read receipts and button taps give you real engagement data rather than an inference from a link click. Second, conversation: suggested replies turn a broadcast into a two-way exchange — confirm, reschedule, track, pay — handled inside the thread. Third, fallback: not every recipient will be RCS-capable on every device at every moment, so a serious deployment always defines an SMS fallback, and you should ask any provider exactly how theirs works and how it is billed.

What does not change is compliance. Consent, opt-out handling and the rules about what you may send still apply, and for US traffic your fallback SMS still runs on registered 10DLC or toll-free routes. If that side is not yet in place, start with our A2P 10DLC requirements guide.

Reach: is it worth it yet?

This was the honest objection until recently, and it has largely dissolved. RCS is available on Android by default through Google Messages, and Apple's addition of RCS in iOS 18 brought iPhone users on the major US carriers into the same channel. Industry estimates now put RCS reach above 80% of smartphone users in mature markets such as the US, with over a billion RCS messages a day in the US alone as of 2025. Reach still varies by country and carrier, so the sensible check before committing a programme is a capability lookup across your own contact list rather than a market-level statistic.

What it costs

RCS is priced per message, and rates differ by carrier and by message type — a basic text-only RCS message costs less than a rich-media one, and inbound is usually cheaper than outbound. It is more expensive per message than SMS. Whether that is worth it depends entirely on the programme: for a one-way reminder that people already act on, probably not; for anything involving a decision, a payment, a reschedule or a fraud-sensitive confirmation, the completion rate usually more than covers the difference. Getting started: what is actually involved

  1. Brand and sender verification. Google verifies the brand behind an RCS sender, which means submitting your business details, logo and the use case. This is separate from 10DLC registration and takes its own review cycle.
  2. Agent setup. Your sender profile — name, logo, colour, description, contact details — is configured and reviewed.
  3. Carrier launch. The agent is enabled with the carriers you need, which is where timelines vary most.
  4. Fallback design. Decide what happens when a recipient is not RCS-capable, and make sure the SMS version of the message still makes sense on its own.

Most of the delay in an RCS launch is verification and carrier enablement, not engineering. A provider that handles those for you, rather than pointing you at a portal, is worth more than a fractionally better rate — see which RCS providers handle Google sender verification and 10DLC for you.

When RCS is the right choice — and when it is not

Good fits: order and delivery updates with tracking buttons, appointment confirmation and rescheduling, identity-sensitive alerts where a customer needs to trust the sender, product browsing or booking flows that would otherwise need an app, and anything where you currently lose people to "is this real?". Poor fits: bulk one-way blasts where SMS already performs, markets or carriers with thin RCS support, and any programme whose value does not depend on the customer doing something in return.

People also ask

What is RCS business messaging?

RCS business messaging is the business channel within Rich Communication Services, the successor to SMS in a phone's default messaging app. Messages come from a verified sender showing your brand name and logo, and can include images, rich cards, carousels and tappable reply buttons, with delivery and read receipts reported back to the sender. It requires no app download on the recipient's side.

What is the difference between RCS and SMS?

SMS is plain text limited to 160 characters per segment, sent from an anonymous phone number with no read receipts. RCS carries verified branding, rich media, carousels and interactive buttons, has no 160-character limit, and reports delivery and read status. RCS runs over data through the carrier's RCS network, so a fallback to SMS is used when a recipient is not RCS-capable.

Does the iPhone support RCS business messaging?

Yes. Apple added RCS support in iOS 18 in late 2024, and RCS for business is supported on iPhone through the major US carriers including AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon, as well as a number of international carriers. iOS 18 or later is required, and exact feature availability still varies by carrier.

Is RCS business messaging free?

No. It is billed per message, with rates varying by carrier and by whether the message is basic or rich media, and inbound generally cheaper than outbound. It costs more per message than SMS, so it earns its place on programmes where the interaction matters — payments, rescheduling, delivery choices, fraud-sensitive confirmations — rather than on plain one-way blasts.

Do I still need 10DLC registration if I use RCS?

For US traffic, yes in practice. RCS has its own Google brand and sender verification process, but your SMS fallback still travels over standard US messaging routes, which means registered 10DLC or toll-free numbers. Treat the two as parallel requirements rather than alternatives.
