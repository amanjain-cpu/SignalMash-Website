SIP vs VoIP: What's the Difference and Which Do You Need? (2026)

Quick answer: SIP vs VoIP

VoIP (voice over IP) is the technology of carrying phone calls as data packets over an IP network. SIP (Session Initiation Protocol) is the signalling standard most VoIP systems use to set up, manage and end those calls. They are not competitors: almost every VoIP call you make uses SIP underneath. The confusion comes from the products built on them. "Hosted VoIP" usually means a cloud phone system sold per user, where the provider runs everything; "SIP trunking" means you keep your own phone system and buy only the connection to the public telephone network, billed per minute or per channel. So the real question is not SIP versus VoIP but hosted phone system versus your own PBX on a SIP trunk.

Three layers, three words

It helps to picture the stack.

  • VoIP is the layer. Any call that travels as IP packets instead of over a dedicated circuit is VoIP: a softphone on a laptop, a desk phone plugged into the office network, a contact-centre platform, a consumer app. VoIP says nothing about who runs the system or how it reaches the outside world.
  • SIP is the language. Defined in IETF RFC 3261, SIP handles the conversation about a call: INVITE (someone wants to talk), 180 Ringing, 200 OK (answered), BYE (hang up), plus the negotiation of which audio codec both ends will use. The voice itself travels separately as RTP packets. Other signalling protocols exist (H.323 in older video systems, proprietary protocols inside some vendors' products), but SIP won, which is why phones, PBXs and carriers from different vendors interoperate at all.
  • SIP trunking is the product. A SIP trunk is a SIP connection between your phone system and a carrier that carries your calls to and from the public telephone network. It replaces the PRI or analogue lines a carrier used to install. You buy it when you already own the phone system.

So a hosted VoIP seat and a SIP trunk both use VoIP and both speak SIP. The difference is where the phone system lives and what you pay for.

When hosted VoIP is the right answer

If nobody in the company wants to own a phone system, a hosted service is simpler: sign up, assign users, plug in phones. The per-seat model works when call volume per person is modest, and the bundled features (video meetings, chat, mobile apps) are often the real reason to buy. The trade-off appears when volume rises: a 50-agent team making outbound calls all day will pay far more per month in seat licences and overage than the same minutes on a trunk.

When SIP trunking is the right answer

Three situations make a SIP trunk the obvious choice. You already run a PBX or a contact-centre platform and you like it; you only need the carrier connection. Your call volume is high enough that per-minute pricing beats per-seat pricing, which for outbound-heavy teams happens quickly at rates of $0.0068 a minute or less. Or you are connecting a platform that expects to bring its own carrier, such as a CCaaS product on BYOC or Microsoft Teams through Direct Routing. In each case the trunk is elastic (capacity grows with demand), provisioned in hours, and portable between sites because it lives on the internet rather than in a wiring closet. The pricing guide works through the break-even arithmetic.

Can you use both?

Yes, and many companies do. A common pattern is a hosted system for office staff and a SIP trunk feeding the contact-centre platform, or a hosted system for internal features with a SIP trunk underneath it via BYOC so the minutes are billed by the carrier rather than marked up by the software vendor. The two are layers, not rivals.

Quality, security and the things people worry about

Call quality on either model depends on the same three network numbers: latency, jitter and packet loss. Prioritise voice traffic on your network (QoS), give each G.711 call roughly 87 to 100 kbps in each direction, and both hosted VoIP and SIP trunks sound like a landline. Security is a matter of the provider's practice rather than the model: look for TLS on signalling, SRTP on media, IP allow-lists or registration credentials on the trunk, and fraud controls on international dialling. Compliance obligations such as E911 and STIR/SHAKEN apply to both; the difference is that on a trunk you are responsible for feeding the PBX correct emergency-address data, which the E911 and STIR/SHAKEN guide covers.

People also ask

Is SIP the same as VoIP?

No. VoIP is the general technology of carrying calls over IP networks; SIP is the signalling protocol most VoIP systems use to set up, manage and end calls. Nearly all business VoIP calls use SIP, so the two are complementary rather than alternatives.

What is the difference between SIP trunking and hosted VoIP?

With hosted VoIP the provider runs the phone system and sells you seats per user. With SIP trunking you keep your own PBX or contact-centre platform and buy only the connection to the public telephone network, billed per minute or per channel. SIP trunking suits call-heavy operations and businesses that already own a phone system; hosted VoIP suits offices that want phones without managing one.

Do I need a SIP trunk if I use VoIP?

Only if you run your own phone system. A hosted VoIP service includes the carrier connection in the seat price. If you operate a PBX, an on-premises or cloud contact-centre platform, or Microsoft Teams via Direct Routing, you need a SIP trunk to reach the public telephone network.

Is SIP trunking cheaper than VoIP?

For call-heavy use, usually yes: a SIP trunk bills per minute (Signalmash lists $0.0068 outbound in the US) with no per-user licence, while hosted VoIP charges per seat whether or not the seat makes calls. For a small office with light calling, the hosted seat is often cheaper overall because it bundles features and minutes.

What protocol does VoIP use?

Most VoIP systems use SIP for signalling and RTP for the audio stream, with codecs such as G.711, G.729 or Opus encoding the voice. Older systems used H.323 and some vendors use proprietary signalling inside their own products, but SIP is the standard between systems and carriers.
